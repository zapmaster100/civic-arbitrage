# GAME SYSTEMS

## 1. Shared-city externality engine

Civic Arbitrage is built around one shared map. A zoning decision can
simultaneously: 1. increase the owner's parcel value; 2. increase
neighbouring parcels' values regardless of owner; 3. change the Council
bag for future applications nearby; 4. make higher-density development
politically more feasible; 5. eventually change built-neighbour
prerequisites; 6. advance Residential/Commercial growth; 7. trigger
Municipal projects; 8. change the endgame clock.

This is the game's primary interaction system. There is intentionally
little/no direct player-to-player action.

## 2. Ownership-token economy

Each player has six ownership tokens. A token committed to a parcel
cannot be used elsewhere until the parcel is sold/released.

This creates an opportunity cost for land banking. A speculative player
can occupy waterfront or future-growth parcels, but risks having tokens
stranded where the city does not grow.

The desired tension: - hold and benefit from others' development; - sell
and recover liquidity/token; - redeploy to a more underpriced parcel.

## 3. Zoning as equity

Zoning is persistent land value. It is not the same as the building.

Intensity is 0/1/2/3. Parcel value is locally networked: own zoning plus
orthogonal neighbours. Therefore zoning creates externalities.

A parcel can be highly zoned while having no building or a lower-density
building. This is deliberate: entitlement speculation is a valid
strategy.

## 4. Council as probabilistic political feasibility

Council is not intended to be a random event detached from the board.
Players create the odds by shaping the surrounding zoning.

For each of four orthogonal neighbours: - 2 cubes from density-distance
comparison; - 1 cube from use match/mismatch.

This gives exactly 12 Yes/No cubes. Draw 7; majority passes.

The system creates: - impossible/very poor applications in incompatible
contexts; - toss-ups at mixed contexts; - increasingly reliable
applications in compatible neighbourhoods.

Redraw cost scales by proposed density, turning political persistence
into an economic decision.

## 5. Building as cashflow

The latest design separates **equity** and **cashflow**: - zoning
creates stored property value; - building pays cash immediately.

The equal-payout direction is meant to prevent High from being an
automatic jackpot and to make exiting at Low/Medium economically
legitimate.

A player choosing Low → Medium → High can collect multiple building
payouts but spends more actions and takes more Council/market risk. A
player jumping directly to High saves actions but forfeits the skipped
building payouts.

## 6. Market as self-correcting subsidy

Two rows of five create a spatial price queue.

Taking a deeper card puts \$1 on each skipped card. Unwanted cards
accumulate money. Eventually the subsidy makes an unattractive
building/card worth taking.

Crucially, the current decision allows a player to take a card **only
for its accumulated cash and discard the building**. This is a
deliberate pressure-release valve.

If the player can actually build it, the same card is more lucrative
because they receive both the subsidy and the base building payout.

This is intended to reward development readiness without blocking
market-clearing behavior.

## 7. Building prerequisites as urban interdependence

Low buildings are broadly accessible. Higher buildings need built
opposite-use support: - Residential needs Commercial one level below; -
Commercial needs Residential one level below.

This makes the *built* city matter separately from zoning.

A player's construction can enable an opponent's project. This is
another externality and a source of indirect interaction.

## 8. Roads/frontage as infrastructure externality

Roads do not gate zoning. They gate construction through frontage.

This allows speculative entitlement of inaccessible land while
preserving the need for infrastructure before development.

Roads are shared geography; extending one for yourself can unlock
opponents' parcels.

Waterfront can contribute frontage, but exact frontage tables are not
fully resolved.

## 9. Waterfront as value-only automata neighbour

Ocean and river frontage is not zoning and remains Greenfield
politically.

For valuation only, a marked waterfront edge mirrors the adjacent
parcel's own zoning value. This makes a waterfront parcel's zoning more
valuable without giving it easier Council odds.

This was intentionally preferred over a flat +\$1 or acquisition
surcharge because: - it creates value only after the player creates
zoning; - it scales naturally with intensity; - it uses the existing
zoning-value language; - it resembles another neighbour increasing the
parcel's value; - it preserves speculation rather than taxing it away.

Working board reminder: `≋$` (waves + value).

## 10. Residential/Commercial growth balance

Private development removes/advances Residential and Commercial growth
cubes.

One side may spill into the next level if a project needs more cubes
than remain in the current level, but that type then locks until the
other side catches up.

This prevents spiteful timing where a player refuses to finish the final
cube because they want to control access to the Municipal card.

When both sides complete the level, a Municipal building unlocks
immediately.

## 11. Municipal buildings as pacing and endgame

Municipal projects: - are triggered by balanced city growth; - enter a
special market staging position; - cannot be discarded; - ultimately
must be built; - the final one ends the game.

They therefore turn shared development into a game clock. Players can
indirectly rush or delay the ending based on their hidden wealth and
remaining property upside.

Exact Municipal dataset and payout/requirements are not fully recovered
and must not be invented.

## 12. Hidden-money scoring

Cash is hidden; the city is public.

This is intended to avoid a discouraging public score track and create
an endgame reveal. Players can see who owns valuable parcels and observe
transactions, but cannot perfectly track spending on redraws, market
access, acquisitions, etc.

Final wealth is cash plus remaining parcel liquidation value.

## 13. Visual information system

The physical design intentionally strips information once it is no
longer relevant.

Zoning tile: - use/color; - 1/2/3 circles; - visible strip on right
under the building.

Building tile: - facade only / density conveyed by height; - no
persistent dollar value under latest system.

Market/back side: - all information required before construction: use,
density, frontage, neighbour prerequisite, current/base payout as
needed, plus market subsidy tokens placed physically/digitally.

This supports a dense finished city without icon overload.
